01 / About

Signal, not noise

I'm currently at the Wu Tsai Human Performance Alliance at Stanford, where I build individual-level models of physiological adaptation from longitudinal wearable and training data, and led a cohort-scale study on menstrual cycle physiology and sleep. I also manage the Alliance's external partnerships, run the Agility Projects program ($1M in annual funding), and keep track of its research portfolio across Stanford.

I studied electrical engineering at the University of Puerto Rico at Mayagüez (control systems), then at Stanford (M.Sc. in signal processing and machine learning), where I completed my PhD with support from NSF, Sloan, and NIH fellowships. My background also includes postdoctoral work on hippocampal and parahippocampal circuits in rodents, supported by an NIH NRSA fellowship and funded in part by the Simons Foundation, and signal processing consulting for FDA-regulated cardiac devices.

The right model starts with the right scientific question.

02 / Research Areas

What I work on

Performance

Physiological adaptation & performance

Training response, recovery, and injury risk from longitudinal wearable data: heat and altitude effects, sex differences in bone stress injury and race performance, energy-availability markers in ultramarathoners, and injury mechanisms in elite athletes.

Physiology

Menstrual cycle biology

Cohort-scale normative physiology, sleep, and cycle variability from wearable biometrics: 42,759 cycles in 2,596 women across the adult lifespan (npj Digital Medicine, 2026).

Neuroscience

Neural coding & memory

How hippocampal and parahippocampal circuits represent space and experience, and how human cortex supports memory, from single neurons to intracranial recordings.

03 / Technical Expertise

Toolbox

Causal inference

Wearable data is observational, unbalanced, and missing nights. Natural experiments, state-space models, and double-robust ML pull causal answers out of it anyway.

Example: sleep-phase natural experiment

ML for biology

Off-the-shelf architectures rarely match biological questions. I design encoder models and custom architectures where the hypothesis is built into the model itself.

Example: eLife 2024 · encoder-decoder notebook

Signal processing

Biological signals bury structure under artifact. Adaptive filtering, multi-band decomposition, and real-time event detection, from EEG-fMRI to an FDA-regulated wearable defibrillator.

Example: EEG-fMRI preprocessing code

Decoding

What does a population of neurons know, and when? Representational similarity analyses and population-level decoders, from single units to intracranial EEG.

Example: PNAS 2015 · eLife 2024

Agentic AI

AI agents are only as rigorous as the structure around them. I set the scientific specification and build research repositories with Claude Code that absorb repeated experimental iterations without sprawl, with claims settled by executed checks.

Data visualization

A figure is an argument. Publication and talk figures designed around the claim they carry, generated from tested, reproducible code.

Example: npj Digital Medicine 2026 figures

Eval-driven discovery

As an early tester of Google Labs' Computational Discovery (AlphaEvolve), I use eval-driven, score-optimizing code generation to surface novel feature engineering and hierarchical models for athletic performance and injury-risk prediction.

04 / Open Science

Reproducible by design

I try to make my research auditable end to end: not just sharing code, but sharing it in a form where every figure and statistic can be traced back to the data.

npj Digital Medicine, 2026

Fully reproducible analysis, from raw biometrics to published figures

The menstrual cycle paper ships with dedicated figure-generation code, every statistic worked through in Jupyter notebooks, and the API built to generate those figures and statistics, alongside a companion website. Browse the repo, or see an example notebook: the sleep-phase natural experiment.
